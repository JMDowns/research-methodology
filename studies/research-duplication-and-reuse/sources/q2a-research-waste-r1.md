# Q2 strand A: how research communities avoid duplicated work (round 1)

Gathered 2026-11-08. Facts marked [S] come from search snippets only. "(unverified)" means not confirmed on a fetched page.
Where noted, abstracts were read through Europe PMC.

## Headline findings

1. **About 85% of health research effort is lost to waste (estimate, not a measurement).** The authors' own derivation: of 100 projects, 50 get published, 25 of those are usably reported, about 12.5 of those have no serious avoidable design flaw. That is 87.5% waste, rounded to 85%. The 2009 figure did NOT include waste from picking the wrong questions or duplicating work.
2. **Overlap is common among meta-analyses.** Siontis 2013: 49 of 73 meta-analyses from 2010 (67%) had at least one overlapping meta-analysis. Median 2 per topic, max 13. In 20 sampled pairs, 13 later meta-analyses added no new outcome.
3. **Registration does not reliably stop duplicates.** In 1,054 COVID-19 PROSPERO records, 138 (about 13%) were duplicates of earlier work. Of 18 survey respondents who said their work was "not similar", 8 said their registry search found nothing. A 2023 paper says the claim "registration reduces duplication" is still a hypothesis "that remains to be confirmed".
4. **New studies rarely build on earlier ones.** Meta-research cited by Lund: only 21% of earlier similar studies are referenced on average (3 studies pooled); a 2022 scoping review of 69 meta-research studies found "high redundancy and low efforts to minimise it". No study found that measured the effect of the "evidence-based research" fix itself.
5. **Update-in-place is feasible but its upkeep is uneven and its gain is not measured.** Six living reviews reported author-team effort from about 5 minutes to 32 hours per month. No cost-saving figure was found. Without updating, 23% of reviews have a signal that their conclusions are outdated within 2 years (Shojania).

## 1. The research-waste literature

**What it is.** Chalmers and Glasziou 2009 (Lancet 374:86-89) argued that waste happens at four stages: choosing questions, design and methods, publication, report quality. The 2014 Lancet series "Research: increasing value, reducing waste" (5 papers) extended it.

**Measured numbers.**
- Derivation of 85%, from the authors' 2016 BMJ blog (fetched): about 50% of completed trials never published in full (Ross 2012); at least 50% of published reports not usable (Glasziou 2014); at least 50% not systematically informed by prior research (Yordanov 2015). 100 -> 50 -> 25 -> 12.5.
- The blog states the estimate used "at least 50%" as exactly 50%, assumes the stages are independent, and rests mainly on clinical (trial) data. Dollar figures differ: the blog implies about $170 billion of about $200 billion a year. A health-journalism article says $240 billion budget [S].
- The blog says up to about 75% of the waste might be recovered by salvage and repair, but poor design cannot be fixed afterwards.
- Chalmers et al. 2014 Lancet 383:156-165 (abstract via search snippet [S]): four recommendations. Funders should make priority-setting transparent; there should be "systematic assessment of existing evidence before new research is funded"; and better sources of information on ongoing research. Funders hold primary responsibility for waste from choosing what to research.

**Evidence-based research (EBR), Lund et al.**
- Idea: justify and design every new study by a systematic review of earlier relevant studies. EBR Network formed 2014; EU COST Action EVBRES from Oct 2018 (Elsevier page, fetched).
- Problem numbers (Elsevier page, fetched): on average only 21% of earlier similar studies are cited by newer studies (three meta-research studies pooled; Robinson 2011 "Prior Research Citation Index"); under 45% of newer studies cite earlier similar studies at all; median 2 cited regardless of how many exist. In a 2014 investigation, 73% of 136 new studies cited the systematic review that said no more research was needed (Elsevier wording is that they "referred to" it; the snippet reading is ambiguous on whether they still went ahead).
- Scoping review (Lund et al., Syst Rev 2022, 11:241, abstract fetched): 69 meta-research studies; 34 (49%) measured redundancy, 42 (61%) measured use of earlier studies; only 2 of 9 health domains covered. Conclusion: "clear indication of a high prevalence of redundancy and a low prevalence of trying to minimise or avoid redundancy".
- Lund 2021 Paper 2 (J Clin Epidemiol 129:158-166, abstract via Europe PMC): the main method is a systematic review of earlier studies before the new study. The abstract gives no numbers.

**Effect of the fix.** None measured in anything I fetched. The EBR papers describe an ideal process and its challenges.
**Quality risk.** Not discussed. The cost is the time to run a prior-evidence review before each study; no cost figure found.
**Upkeep.** Per-study, ongoing. Needs funders and editors to check it (Elsevier page lists four checks for editors and reviewers).

## 2. Duplicate and overlapping reviews, and registration

**Siontis et al. BMJ 2013** (abstract via Europe PMC, plus a second search snippet): 5% random sample of 2010 meta-analyses; 73 eligible; 49 (67%) had at least one overlapping; median 2 per topic (IQR 1-4); max 13; in 17 topics at least one author was on two or more of the overlapping ones; 13 of 20 sampled later meta-analyses had no additional outcome. Authors still saw some value in independent replication.

**Ioannidis, Milbank Q 2016** (abstract page fetched; PMC copy blocked): PubMed tags 266,782 "systematic reviews" and 58,611 "meta-analyses" (1986 to Dec 2015). Annual output rose 2,728% (SRs) and 2,635% (MAs) from 1991 to 2014, versus 153% for all PubMed items. 185 meta-analyses of antidepressants for depression in 2007-2014. Some topics have more than 20 meta-analyses. Conclusion is hedged: "possibly" the large majority are unnecessary, misleading or conflicted.

**Moher BMJ 2013, via Puljak/Lund 2023** (PMC10071231 fetched): two or three reviews on one topic with similar criteria may be reasonable; four or more likely too many. A new review can be justified if the old one used poor methods.

**Does PROSPERO reduce duplication?**
- Stated aim: reduce unplanned duplication and reporting bias [S]. Duplication is allowed with a reason, such as an out-of-date or weak earlier review [S].
- Only direct measure found: the COVID-19 study (BMJ Open 2022, PMC9716408, fetched). 1,054 records, March 2020 to Jan 2021. 574 with similar titles checked; 195 with similar PICOS; 138 classed duplicates (about 13%). Treatment topics 20%, epidemiology 7.4%, rehabilitation 0. Up to 14 duplicates on one question (hydroxychloroquine). Survey of 138 authors, 41 replies (30%). 85 of 138 had been marked "not similar" at screening. Of those 21 "sufficiently different" respondents, all had found some or all similar reviews, and none justified the difference convincingly. Authors suggest mandatory justification, automated flagging of similar records, and better search tools.
- Puljak/Lund 2023 (fetched): registration reducing duplicates "remains to be confirmed"; 32% of SRs published in 2018 were in PROSPERO; some entries waited up to 6 months to be published; entries waiting over 30 days are now published unchecked. Their prevention list (11 items) includes automated duplicate checks at registration, removing abandoned "zombie" registrations, editors rejecting duplicates, and living reviews.
- Caveat: no before/after or controlled study of PROSPERO was found. This is a gap.

**Effect on quality.** Duplicates can disagree; discordant reviews allow cherry-picking (Puljak/Lund). Replication is sometimes wanted.
**Upkeep.** Registry needs screening staff; the 6-month delays and the 30-day auto-publish rule show the screening load.

## 3. Topic ownership (Cochrane review groups)

**What it is.** Each Cochrane Review Group (CRG) owns a field. Authors register a title with the responsible CRG before starting. The registration process "avoids duplication between different Cochrane Reviews" and checks scope and author skills (Cochrane Oral Health editorial process document [S]). EPOC's rule: a proposed review should not overlap significantly with an existing Cochrane title, protocol or review, or be a subset of one [S]. Fertility Regulation: the whole editorial team checks each title for overlap, expertise and feasibility, taking no more than two weeks [S]. Since 2022, proposals go through Editorial Manager and authors state overlap with published Cochrane content [S]. The final title decision rests with the group [S].

**Measured effect.** None found. I found no study counting duplicates inside Cochrane versus outside it. The Cochrane Handbook page I fetched did not cover this (the fetched page had no text on it); everything above is from group documents seen only as snippets. Treat as [S].
**Quality effect.** Not measured. Cochrane protocols and reviews are peer-reviewed in-house, and some reviews took over 8 years from protocol to review (Puljak/Lund, fetched), which hints at the cost of a gatekeeper.
**Upkeep.** Editorial staff per group; two-week title check per proposal at one group.

## 4. Living systematic reviews (LSR)

**What it is.** A review kept continually updated as new evidence appears (Elliott et al., PLoS Med 2014; J Clin Epidemiol 2017 91:23-30).

**Design (Elliott 2014, fetched).**
- Search strategy is maintained and feeds a continuous workflow: screening, extraction, meta-analysis, reporting. Effort is "moderate and ongoing" instead of intense and sporadic.
- Tiered review: no new studies -> only the search date is updated, no further review; new studies that do not change conclusions -> light editorial check; studies that change estimates or conclusions -> rapid but full peer review.
- Statistics: repeated testing inflates false-positive risk; authors suggest sequential or Bayesian methods (they say this is contested).
- Version numbers or dates in citations.
- The paper is a proposal. It gives no cost figures and no evaluation.

**Why update instead of redo (numbers in the 2014 paper, as reported by the fetched page).** Median time from primary study to review publication 2.5 to 6.5 years (neurotrauma, 792 reports, 73 SRs); 23% of non-updated SRs missed new evidence that would change conclusions within 2 years. The second figure matches Shojania (below).

**Shojania et al. (Europe PMC abstract, fetched).** 100 SRs from 1995-2005: median 5.5 years (CI 4.6-7.6) with no signal that an update was needed; 23% had a signal within 2 years; 15% within 1 year; 7% at publication; 57% any signal overall.

**Criteria (Elliott 2017, abstract fetched).** Best where evidence is emerging quickly, current evidence is uncertain, and new studies could change policy. Search snippet adds [S]: the review must be a priority for decisions; "not all systematic reviews need updating". Full criteria list not read (PDF unreadable).

**Measured cost and upkeep (Millard et al. 2019, Syst Rev, via Europe PMC, fetched).** Six LSRs (three Cochrane, three not), 27 participants, monthly time surveys. Team time ranged from about 5 minutes to 32 hours a month. Citations screened ranged from about 3 to 300 a month. Problems: publishing systems built for one-off papers, ongoing workload, no long-term funding. Seen as feasible and acceptable; participants expected long-run time savings, helped by machine learning and Cochrane Crowd. No measured saving. A Cochrane pilot Sept 2017 to Aug 2018 tracked workload monthly, with search frequency from daily/monthly to three-monthly [S].

**Quality risks.** Statistical false positives from repeated looks; team turnover and lost institutional memory (Elliott 2014); publication format. No study comparing LSR quality to conventional reviews was found.

## 5. Evidence and gap maps (Campbell, 3ie)

**What they are.** Systematic visual maps of what evidence exists in a policy domain, usually a matrix of interventions by outcomes, built from a pre-specified search protocol. They show what exists, not what it concludes (Campbell paper PMC8428058 and PMC8356338, both fetched).

**Stated purposes.** Guide users to high-quality research; inform research priorities and funding; define the focus of later reviews; find gaps. "Avoiding duplication" is NOT stated as a purpose in either fetched paper.

**Measured effect.** None. Neither paper assesses impact on decisions or duplication. Examples of use only: UNICEF used a child-welfare mega-map when funding a map on violence against children; homelessness maps underpin three systematic reviews; Uganda's country map used in planning.
**Cost and time.** No cost figures. 3ie maps take about 3 to 6 months and skip critical appraisal of primary studies, which is why they are cheap. Maps older than 2-3 years are less useful; update within 3 years, preferably yearly for fast fields (PMC8428058). Some bodies have update policies with no enforcement.
**Quality risk.** Maps do not appraise study quality, so they cannot replace reading the sources. Saran and White note there is no central repository, so maps are hard to find [S].

## 6. Other mechanisms

**Overviews of reviews and overlap measurement (Pieper et al. 2014, J Clin Epidemiol 67:368-375, abstract via Europe PMC, fetched).** 60 overviews (2009-2011); only 32 mentioned overlap between included reviews. Corrected covered area (CCA) is a number for how much the same primary studies recur across reviews; median CCA 4.0. Overlap is higher in HTA reports. This is a way to measure and report duplication, not prevent it. Cost: a citation matrix per overview.

**Prevention list (Puljak/Lund 2023).** Ideas without tested effect: change PRISMA item 3 to ask about other reviews on the question; automated duplicate check at registration; close topics once evidence is "conclusive"; contact authors of dormant registrations.

**Searched but not found:** studies on reusing search filters, or on shared data-extraction sets, as a way to cut duplicate effort (one search on gap-map duplication and one on PROSPERO impact returned nothing relevant). Marked as a gap for round 2.

## Cross-cutting notes for the repo design

- Every mechanism here is well described; almost none has a measured effect on duplication. The best numbers describe the problem (67% overlap, 13% duplicate registrations, 21% prior citation), not the fix.
- The one direct test of a registry (PROSPERO COVID) found it leaks, mostly because authors' searches missed similar entries or they did not justify differences. Lesson: a registry needs an automated similarity check, not only a search box.
- Gatekeeper models (Cochrane) trade duplication control against slow throughput (title checks of up to two weeks, reviews taking 8+ years).
- Update-in-place (LSR) works only when someone owns the topic and the team has steady effort; tiered review is the quality control.
- No source measured whether quality drops when earlier work is reused. The LSR tiered-review rule (re-review depth depends on whether new evidence changes conclusions) is the nearest idea.
