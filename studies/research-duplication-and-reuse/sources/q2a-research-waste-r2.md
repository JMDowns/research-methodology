# Q2 strand A: how research communities avoid duplicated work (research waste, reviews, registers, living reviews, gap maps)

Gatherer: Sonnet, 2026-11-08. Tags: [S] = search snippet only, not fetched. "(unverified)" = could not confirm. Everything else came from a fetched page.

## Headline findings

1. Duplication of meta-analyses is common but the cost is not measured. Siontis 2013 found 49 of 73 (67%) sampled 2010 meta-analyses of trials had at least one overlapping meta-analysis (median 2 per topic, max 13). Two sources agree (PMC full text + PubMed search record). They call it "a waste of efforts" but also say some replication is useful.
2. Registration alone does not stop duplication. In PROSPERO, 138 of 1,054 COVID-19 reviews (13%) were registered when a similar protocol already existed. 85 of the 138 authors ticked "Not Similar" on the screening question. 48 said theirs was "sufficiently different". Only 5 said repetition was needed. No study was found that compares duplication rates before and after registration (searched, not found).
3. The "85% of research is wasted" figure is a rough multiplication, not a measured rate: 50% unpublished x 50% badly reported x 50% badly designed leaves 12.5% usable (87.5% waste, rounded to 85%). It only counts waste after a study is chosen. It does not count duplicated questions.
4. Starting new work from prior evidence is rarely done, which is the gap "evidence-based research" (EBR) targets. In 15 meta-research studies (1,724 original studies), a mean of 30.7% (95% CI 23.8 to 37.6) put their results in the context of earlier studies. A 2018 study of 622 trials found only 20% cited a systematic review to justify the trial and 44% cited none. I found no measured effect of the EBR approach on duplication or cost.
5. Living reviews (update instead of redo) are feasible but cost steady effort and need funding. In the six-pilot evaluation, author team time ran from 5 minutes to 32 hours per month and 3 to 300 citations screened per month. Participants called them costlier than standard reviews in resources, though they expected long-run time savings. Quality is kept by tiered change review and sequential statistics. No measured duplication saving.

## 1. Research waste and evidence-based research

**What it is.** Chalmers and Glasziou 2009 (Lancet) listed avoidable waste in producing and reporting research. Macleod et al. 2014 (Lancet series "Increasing value, reducing waste") restated their estimate: about 85% of research investment, about $200 billion of 2010 spending, is wasted. The series says this excluded inefficiencies in regulation and management.

**How the 85% was made** (Glasziou and Chalmers, BMJ blog 2016): 100 projects, about 50% never published in full (50 left), about 50% of those not clear and complete enough to use (25 left), about 50% of those without serious avoidable design flaws (12.5 left). Remainder 87.5%, rounded to 85%. The stage numbers come from different studies. Based mainly on clinical trials. Not a measured single rate. The article gives no direct duplication number; its link to duplication is the claim that at least half of new studies are not designed with systematic reference to earlier research.

**Evidence-based research (EBR).** EVBRES network (EU COST action, from 2018). Idea: review existing evidence systematically before a new study, use it to justify and design the study, and place results in context afterward. The EBR network page (fetched) gives these gap figures:
- A 2011 study: trials cited a median of 2 earlier trials even when 10 or more were available; about half cited none or one.
- A 2018 study of 622 RCTs (2014 to 2016): 20% cited a systematic review as justification, 44% cited none.
- A 2015 study of funding applications: 89% (2006-08) and 100% (2013) cited a systematic review, but over 90% did not use it to inform design beyond justifying the comparison.
- A 2013 study: only 3% of RCTs included an updated systematic review with their results; 37% made any systematic attempt to put results in context.
- Draborg et al. 2022 (Systematic Reviews; abstract read from the fetched PDF): 15 meta-research studies, 1,724 original studies, mean 30.7% placed results in context; heterogeneity I2 = 87.4%; only 1 integrated results in a meta-analysis, 4 in a systematic review. The authors say interpret with caution.

**Measured effect on duplication or effort:** none found. These studies measure how often researchers do the check, not what happens when they do. The EBR page does not quantify waste or redundant trials itself.
**Quality effect/risk:** none reported; the argument is that skipping the check is "unethical, unscientific, and wasteful" (EBR page wording).
**Upkeep:** per-study cost of a prior-evidence search; not quantified in any page I fetched.
Not fetched: Lund et al. 2020 J Clin Epidemiol 129:158-166 and the Chalmers and Glasziou 2009 abstract itself. Cited second-hand via the BMJ blog and the Lancet 2014 text.

## 2. Duplicate and overlapping reviews, and registration

**Siontis, Hernandez-Boussard, Ioannidis, BMJ 2013;347:f4501.** Random 5% sample of 2010 meta-analyses of randomised trials of medical interventions; 73 eligible; searched PubMed to Feb 2013 for overlaps. 49 of 73 (67%) had at least one overlapping meta-analysis; median 2 per topic (IQR 1-4); max 13. No index-review trait predicted overlap. Authors suggest better coordination between reviewers, possibly registering protocols, and acknowledging earlier meta-analyses in PRISMA. [Fetched PMC3716360; second domain: the PubMed record/DOI listing in the search results, [S].] An accompanying BMJ editorial read it more mildly (duplication not a major problem) [S].

**Ioannidis 2016, Milbank Quarterly 94(3):485-514, "Mass production of redundant, misleading, and conflicted systematic reviews and meta-analyses".** Fetched Milbank page: PubMed annual output rose 2,728% (systematic reviews) and 2,635% (meta-analyses) from 1991 to 2014, versus 153% for all PubMed items. Cumulative tags 1986 to Dec 2015: 266,782 systematic reviews, 58,611 meta-analyses. Some topics have more than 20 meta-analyses of the same question; 185 meta-analyses of antidepressants for depression in 2007 to 2014. Concludes few are both non-misleading and useful; most "may be unnecessary, misleading, or conflicted." The page gave no numeric "useful" share. The 2014 counts (28,959 systematic reviews, 9,135 meta-analyses) are [S]. The fetched text did not mention registration or coordination remedies (the page text was partial).

**Moher 2013 BMJ commentary "The problem of duplicate systematic reviews"** (via EASE summary, fetched): two or three reviews on a topic are usually reasonable. Best remedy proposed: make it compulsory to look for existing reviews or protocols before starting. The page reports no data on whether registration reduces duplication.

**PROSPERO (registry of review protocols).** Stated aims: reduce unplanned duplication, add transparency, limit reporting bias [S]. Measured evidence of effect: none found. The closest measurement is Beresford et al., BMJ Open 2022;12:e061862 (read from the fetched PDF):
- 1,054 COVID-19 reviews registered Mar 2020 to Jan 2021; 138 submitted when at least one similar protocol already existed (about 13%).
- Duplication was highest for treatments: 14 reviews on hydroxychloroquine, 7 tocilizumab, 6 remdesivir.
- 85 of the 138 authors answered "Not Similar" on the screening question; 48 said "sufficiently different"; 5 said repetition was needed.
- Survey: 41 responses from 138 invited. Reasons: different PICOS or planned analyses (13), poor quality of earlier registrations (2), need to update (3). Of 18 who said "not similar", 8 said they searched PROSPERO and found nothing; some search terms were very broad (e.g. "COVID-19" alone).
- Limits: one topic during a pandemic surge; only 4 of 17 topic areas assessed for duplication; 30% survey response.
Reading: a register works only if people search it well and the "is it similar" test is applied honestly. Upkeep: registry staff plus a screening question at entry. Quality risk: none reported; the cost shown is wasted reviews, not worse reviews.

## 3. Topic ownership (Cochrane review groups)

All from a search summary, not fetched: [S] Cochrane requires every proposed review to be agreed with a review group and registered as a title in its system (Archie). Titles registered there are the main duplicate check. For a topic spanning two groups, a Host group registers the title and tells the Non-host group, which links it to its topics list. Unresolved disputes go to the Editor in Chief. Groups can decline topics outside their priorities. A 2017 abstract said title registration forms varied by group and were a barrier for non-English speakers [S]. Measured duplicate rate under this system: not found. Upkeep: editor/managing-editor staff per group (no numbers found). I did not fetch any Cochrane documentation page, so treat this section as (unverified) pending a fetch of the Cochrane "proposing and registering new reviews" guidance.

## 4. Living systematic reviews (update instead of redo)

**Elliott et al. 2014, PLoS Med 11(2):e1001603 (fetched from the PLoS page).** A living review is a high-quality, online-only summary updated rapidly and frequently as new studies appear. Best for controlled-trial questions in fast-moving areas and high-priority questions. Needs: continuous workflow (maintained search, screening, extraction, meta-analysis, reporting), long-term author management with memory, workflow tools, shared data repositories. Machine learning could cut manual screening by up to 50% in new reviews and over 90% in updates (their claim). No cost figures given. Quality controls: formal sequential or Bayesian monitoring to limit false positives; tiered change handling (no new studies: update search date only; negligible impact: editorial review; significant impact: fast but full peer review); version numbers and access dates. Risks: repeated testing inflates false positives; unstable early estimates; fragmented tools; hard to sustain author teams; contributor bias in crowd screening.

**Elliott et al. 2017, J Clin Epidemiol 91:23-30, "Living systematic review: 1. Introduction".** Confirmed as a published paper (title, journal, pages) from search results [S]. Its abstract was not fetched; its criteria for when to go living are (unverified).

**Millard et al. 2019, Systematic Reviews, mixed-methods evaluation of 6 pilot living reviews (3 Cochrane, 3 not; 9 publications) (fetched PMC6911272).** 27 interviewees plus monthly time surveys.
- Citations screened per month: 3 to 300.
- Author time: 5 minutes to 32 hours per month. Managing editors 0 to 3.5 hours; information specialists 30 minutes to 6 hours. Searches monthly to every 3 months.
- Baseline reviews had 7 to 101 studies; 0 to 96 new studies found per review during the pilot. Only one review was formally republished, twice.
- Five of six had some personnel funding. Participants called living reviews more costly than standard ones in resources but expected time savings in the long run.
- Risks: dependence on a few people, publication and copyright delays, rushed editing under tight deadlines, unclear authorship, machine classifiers less useful for non-randomised designs. Small sample; interviewers were part of the pilot. Time data hard to interpret because searches covered several reviews.
No measured effect on duplication of other teams' reviews. Not found: any post-2019 evaluation (I did not search for one).

## 5. Evidence and gap maps

**What they are** (Campbell Systematic Reviews editorial, PMC8356338, fetched): systematic, visual maps (usually a matrix of interventions by outcomes) of what research exists in a broad topic. Stated purposes: guide users to good research, inform research priority setting, define focus for later synthesis. The page does not mention duplication. First two Campbell maps: 396 studies (agroforestry) and 166 studies (disability interventions in low- and middle-income countries). Caveats: different agencies use different approaches; most maps show what exists, not what it says; framework design is the hardest step. Upkeep: "desirable" to have a plan to update annually.

**3ie** (fetched 3ieimpact.org page): the page lists 43 maps (my count of entries). It says maps show funders and researchers "where more investments are needed or where they can avoid duplicating existing research." Maps are updated (examples: a map first published 2022 updated 2024; one updating a 2015 map). Creation involves stakeholder consultation, a systematic search of published and unpublished work, and including ongoing studies.

**Measured effect on duplication:** none found. The claim is a stated purpose. A funder used a map as input to strategy [S]. A 2018 review noted maps are hard to find because there is no repository [S, Saran and White]. Cost of a map: not found.

## 6. Other mechanisms

**Overviews of reviews / umbrella reviews.** Pieper et al. 2014 (J Clin Epidemiol) proposed the corrected covered area (CCA), a score for how much the same primary studies recur across the reviews in an overview. Of 60 overviews (2009 to 2011), 32 reported overlap and the median CCA was 4.0 [S, via search summary; full text not fetched]. Example bands from a later source: 0 to 5% slight, 6 to 10% moderate, 11 to 15% high, over 15% very high (example only) [S]. Relevance here: an overlap score is a cheap, measurable way to quantify duplication between two documents' source lists. Effect on reducing duplication: not found.

**Compulsory prior-review check.** Moher 2013 and Siontis 2013 both propose making it mandatory to look for existing reviews first. No measured effect found.

**Not covered by this strand (no evidence gathered):** search-filter reuse, shared data extraction repositories (only mentioned as an enabler in Elliott 2014), Zettelkasten and wiki merge practices (assigned elsewhere or to strand B).

## Takeaways for the repo design (from the gathered evidence only)

- Communities mostly rely on a pre-start search plus a register. The evidence says the register fails when the search is weak or the "similar" test is self-judged (PROSPERO COVID data).
- Duplicate topics show up where interest is highest (hydroxychloroquine 14 reviews; some meta-analysis topics over 20). A repo would expect clusters too.
- Overlap can be scored with a cheap measure (CCA idea) rather than judged by feel.
- Reuse by updating (living review) kept quality through explicit tiers of review depth by change size, and cost steady upkeep. Funding was the main sustainability risk.
- Nobody here measured the quality cost of de-duplication directly. The reported risks are false positives from repeated updating and rushed editing.
