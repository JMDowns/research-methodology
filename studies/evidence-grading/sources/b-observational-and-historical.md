# B. Observational and historical evidence (Q2 rows: natural experiments, comparative databases, archaeology, ethnography, written sources)

Gathered 2026-11-08 by a Sonnet researcher under the R3 rules. Marks: [S] = from a search snippet only, not a fetched page. "(unverified)" = not confirmed from a fetched page. Several PDFs were read after saving them locally and running pdftotext; they count as fetched.

## Headline findings

1. **Random assignment in a natural experiment is not enough.** Sekhon and Titiunik examined four published natural experiments (redistricting and the personal vote; House members moving to the Senate; randomized electoral quotas for women in India; regression discontinuity on incumbency). In each, "random assignment of the intervention is not sufficient to provide an unbiased estimate of the causal effect"; extra, problematic assumptions were needed ([Sekhon and Titiunik 2012](https://www.jsekhon.com/papers/SekhonTitiunik.pdf)).
2. **Close-election RD failed its own balance test in one analysis, then matched a lottery benchmark in another.** Caughey and Sekhon found bare winners and bare losers in US House races 1942-2008 differ on pre-election traits ([abstract summary, IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/polals/v19y2011i04p385-408_01.html)). Using Finnish tie-break lotteries as a randomized benchmark, the benchmark showed no personal incumbency advantage, conventional RD showed a moderate significant one, and bias-corrected RD agreed with the lottery ([LSE record, Hyytinen et al.](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/84146)). So the same design can pass or fail depending on setting and estimator.
3. **Moralising gods: the grade-relevant fact is how missing data was coded.** Beheim et al. report 61% of Seshat moralising-gods data points had no value (mostly polities under one million people), the original paper re-coded them as absences, and using recorded data only or standard imputation reverses the finding ([Harvard DASH record of the abstract](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/52b03c4e-e07a-4e7e-b378-242ae8cc0d59); same figures in a search summary of the LSE/IDEAS records). Nature retracted the 2019 paper in July 2021; three of the authors disagreed with the retraction; the authors republished a "retake" in 2022 that says the corrected analysis does not change the main finding (a claim by the original authors, not independent) ([CSH page for the retake](https://csh.ac.at/publication/testing-the-big-gods-hypothesis-with-global-historical-data-a-review-and-retake/); retraction details [S] from a search summary of the retraction note).
4. **Two expert-coded databases disagree, and each side's account comes from a party.** The Database of Religious History (DRH) historians and the Seshat team dispute codes such as Late Shang China. Turchin, who leads Seshat, defends the Seshat codes and calls the DRH coding a failure ([Turchin blog, a party to the dispute](https://peterturchin.com/they-call-it-coding-errors/)). I found no independent referee.
5. **Cross-cultural databases carry built-in time and coding limits.** D-PLACE (over 1,400 societies) draws on the Ethnographic Atlas (1,291 societies) and Binford (339 hunter-gatherer societies). In the Atlas, 69% of focal years fall in 1900-1950 and 25% in the 19th century. Focal years for the same society can differ between datasets by more than 50 years. The authors say coder and ethnographer bias affects the data ([Kirby et al. 2016, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0158391)).
6. **Galton's problem has partial fixes, none complete.** Related societies are not independent samples. Phylogenetic methods fix vertical descent but not borrowing between neighbours; in simulations, type I error rose with horizontal transmission and non-phylogenetic tests had higher error than independent contrasts ([Nunn et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2981906)).
7. **Radiocarbon dates can be ambiguous, and Bayesian modelling can mislead.** For a roughly 20-30 year village site in Ontario, dates on short-lived plant remains gave about equal probability near AD 1500 or AD 1600 because of a plateau in the calibration curve. OxCal's probability can also become "overly compressed" and drift from the real age ([Manning and Birch 2022](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/2119C55BDB0D7ABE3205E766D275FE61/core-reader), read from the open-access PDF, DOI 10.1017/RDC.2022.23).
8. **Ethnographic restudies can disagree for years.** Shankman reports Freeman "cherry picked" evidence against Mead and that unreleased transcripts of Freeman's own interviews with his key informant were contradictory ([CU Boulder news release on Shankman 2009](https://www.colorado.edu/today/2009/12/10/trashing-margaret-meads-reputation-based-deeply-flawed-evidence-cu-prof-finds)). Shankman also says Mead's work was "not flawless". Dissent persists [S].

## Notes

### Natural experiments and quasi-experiments

- Dunning's definition: natural experiments are observational studies where assignment is claimed to be "as-if" random, and "both a priori reasoning and empirical evidence are used to validate the assertion of randomization." Random or as-if random assignment is "the defining feature." He suggests a "continuum of plausibility" of as-if randomness, not a yes/no ([Dunning 2008, QMMR](https://zenodo.org/records/996336/files/Dunning_2008_Natural_QMMR_6_2.pdf), read from the saved PDF).
- Dunning's examples: John Snow's cholera study (water companies mixing within streets, no selection by residents) is the strong case. The 2003 polling-place closures (Brady and McNulty) had some small pre-treatment imbalance on age, and "the assumption of as-if random assignment may not completely stand up," though differences were small relative to the effect (same Dunning 2008 PDF).
- Dunning's book (2012): Chapter 8 is on plausibility of as-if random, Chapter 9 on credibility of the statistical model, Chapter 10 on whether the intervention is relevant to the question [S] (from Cambridge chapter listings in search results; I did not read the chapters).
- Sekhon and Titiunik's two failure routes: the groups the researcher wants to compare are not the groups the intervention makes comparable, or the comparable groups say little about the effect of interest. They name this "a tension between internal validity and substantive relevance." Example: the one-time redistricting design was valid under randomization, but compared groups in different districts, so "too much relevance had to be given up to gain validity, and the design had to be abandoned" ([Sekhon and Titiunik 2012](https://www.jsekhon.com/papers/SekhonTitiunik.pdf)).
- Their India quota reanalysis found "the evidence for the effects of the quotas is weaker than originally reported", even though the treatment was actually randomized (same PDF).
- Credibility revolution framing: Angrist and Pischke (2010) say design-based studies feature real or natural experiments and are valued for prima facie credibility; most common in development, education, labor, health and public finance, rarer in industrial organization and macroeconomics [S] ([LSE abstract via search](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/28001); also [NBER w15794](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15794)). I did not read the full text, so their treatment of IV, RD and difference-in-differences is not covered here.
- Local effects: Sekhon and Titiunik's relevance point is the main check I found. I did not find a fetched source quantifying how often local (complier or at-the-cutoff) effects fail to generalise (unverified).

### Comparative and cross-cultural databases

- **Seshat coding** (the databank's own methods page): variables are a number, a range, or present/absent/unknown, with "inferred present/absent" for indirect evidence; coders give ranges for magnitudes. Research assistants code first, over 100 experts check, and a Data Review Board reviews every code. Disagreement among experts and uncertainty are "recorded where possible" so analyses can use alternative readings. Every datum links to scholarly sources and a narrative note. Polity time series are sampled every 100 years ([Seshat methods](https://seshatdatabank.info/methods)). This is the databank's own description of itself.
- **The moralising-gods episode**, step by step: original Nature paper (2019); Matters Arising by Beheim et al. (Nature 595, E29-E34, 2021) saying 61% missing data was coded as absence ([Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/52b03c4e-e07a-4e7e-b378-242ae8cc0d59)); retraction July 2021 with a note that the authors should have labelled the values "absent" or "inferred absent" rather than "unknown", while three authors (Baines, Covey, Feeney) disagreed [S]; 2022 Religion, Brain & Behavior "retake" saying the main finding stands, using an expanded codebook and a "more direct statistical method" ([CSH](https://csh.ac.at/publication/testing-the-big-gods-hypothesis-with-global-historical-data-a-review-and-retake/)). The retake's society and polity counts were not on the page I fetched (unverified).
- The 2020 DRH-historians commentary (Slingerland and co-authors) objected to the quantitative coding and to the unclear role of expert historians; one example is Susiana, where the dataset codes no moralising god for about two thousand years after an earlier high god is documented [S]. A search snippet also says over two-thirds of Seshat data did not match equivalent DRH entries [S] (from a search summary of the Beheim preprint; the preprint itself returned 403). Seshat's side replies that the critiques misread its methods [S] ([Equinox record](https://api.equinoxpub.com/articles/39422), not fetched).
- **D-PLACE (Kirby et al. 2016):** Ethnographic Atlas 1,291 societies, 90+ coded traits, weighted to North America and Africa; Binford 339 hunter-gatherer societies, 40 variables, about 66% also in the Atlas; totals over 1,400 societies, about 1,202 languages. Atlas and Binford are not merged because "coders, ethnographers, and time/place foci differ" (the two disagree on Pumé subsistence). Galton's problem handling: societies link to Glottolog language trees; computed Bayesian phylogenies exist for nine families (Austronesian, Bantu, Dene-Yeniseian, Indo-European, Japonic, Koreanic, Pama-Nyungan, Semitic, Tukanoan); coordinates allow spatial controls. Stated limits: Glottolog trees are topological with no branch lengths in time; language may not track deep cultural ancestry because of shift and borrowing; society boundaries differ by dataset ([Kirby et al. 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0158391)). Cross-check of the "over 1,400 societies" figure: [Bristol record](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/d-place-a-global-database-of-cultural-linguistic-and-environmenta/).
- **SCCS:** 186 societies (Murdock and White, 1969 per HRAF), each pinned to a specific time and place, built to "minimize cultural relatedness" by taking one society per culture area. HRAF warns that matching societies across samples by name "is likely to increase random error", and that samples may use different time and place foci for the same culture ([HRAF](https://hraf.yale.edu/resources/reference/sccs-cases-in-ehraf)). A search snippet says over 2,000 variables have been coded on it by various researchers and not all cover all 186 societies [S]. I found no inter-coder reliability figures (unverified).
- **eHRAF, Pulotu, DRH coding:** only eHRAF's link to SCCS was covered. Pulotu and DRH structure were not fetched (unverified). Gap.
- **Galton's problem:** the core non-independence point, vertical descent versus horizontal borrowing, and phylogenetic independent contrasts are from [Nunn et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2981906) (simulation paper; their results depend on modelling assumptions such as whether traits transfer in pairs or independently). The retention indices they discuss "may have very low power to detect phylogenesis". Mace and Pagel (1994) as the origin of the phylogenetic proposal, and Korotayev and de Munck's "Galton's opportunity" reframing, are [S] from search summaries. Galton's problem is a design check, not a reason to ignore the database: a claim based on the database should say whether it controlled for relatedness.

### Archaeology

- **Dating.** Radiocarbon dates must be calibrated against a curve. Where the curve has a reversal or plateau (here 1480-1630), one date can mean two calendar ranges. In the Manning and Birch case, short-lived plant remains gave roughly equal probability near 1500 or 1600, the site was originally proposed at about AD 1500-1530, and after more dates and an in-built-age model for charcoal it was placed at about AD 1595-1626 (95.4% probability) ([Manning and Birch 2022](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/2119C55BDB0D7ABE3205E766D275FE61/core-reader)). Their own warning: "dating probability for short-duration sites" can be "overly compressed" under OxCal and "shift away from the real age range." A tree-ring wiggle-match is the best fix where available. This is one site and one period, not a general error rate.
- Other radiocarbon points from search snippets only [S]: community consensus favours probabilistic calibration plus Bayesian modelling for dated sequences; IntCal20 is thin around 350-250 BCE (Kyrenia Ship study); Historic England guidance (Bayliss and Marshall) covers the old-wood effect. The Historic England page returned 403, so I could not read the old-wood definition (unverified).
- **What survives.** Contreras and Codding show landscape processes (geology) decide which sites and materials survive and get found, which biases "dates-as-data" population reconstructions based on counts of radiocarbon dates. They demonstrate, using a simulated case drawing on empirical landform ages from the Coso Basin, that corrections built from local landform-age distributions beat global or no correction ([Contreras and Codding, preprint PDF](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10531818)). That is a simulation, not a measured error rate.
- Introductory sources note stone survives better than wood, cloth or bone, and warn against treating absence of evidence as evidence of absence [S] (study-guide sites; low quality). A search snippet on the Sahul record argues that taphonomy and sampling weaken claims that Pleistocene populations lacked cultural complexity [S].
- **Reading social structure from remains.** I found no fetched source on how reliable inference of social structure from remains is. Gap (unverified).

### Ethnography

- **Mead-Freeman.** Mead's *Coming of Age in Samoa* was published 1928; Freeman challenged it in 1983 and claimed in 1999 that Mead had been hoaxed by informants [S]. Shankman (2009, *The Trashing of Margaret Mead*) found Freeman cherry-picked, found unreleased 1987 and 1993 interview transcripts with the informant Fa'apua'a that were contradictory or inconsistent with Freeman's arguments, and noted Freeman's own graduate thesis called the taupou system "virtually defunct" by the 1940s, in conflict with his published account ([CU Boulder](https://www.colorado.edu/today/2009/12/10/trashing-margaret-meads-reputation-based-deeply-flawed-evidence-cu-prof-finds)). Orans (1996) re-read Mead's field notes at the Library of Congress and argued the data support several conclusions, and that her conclusions rest on interpretation [S] ([search summary of Orans, ANU record](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/handle/1885/114717)). Lesson: both sides drew on the same single-observer record, and the dispute was settled (to the extent it was) by archival material, not by new fieldwork.
- **Ethnographic present.** Fabian's *Time and the Other* (1983) argues anthropology places its subjects in a time other than the observer's, and names the ethnographic present as a main device. Later scholars say he overstates this, and note he offered no concrete remedy [S] (search summary of Wikipedia, a CUNY essay by Birth, and an EASA abstract; not fetched). Direct evidence in the database section: Atlas focal years show the "present" being coded is mostly 1900-1950 (Kirby et al.).
- I did not find a fetched estimate of how often ethnographic restudies disagree (unverified). Two well-known cases is not a rate.

### Historical written sources

- Thin evidence only; every item is [S]. Herodotus gives 1,700,000 fighting men for Xerxes' land army (7.60); scholars widely reject this. Delbrück (1887) argued for at most about 55,000; a historiographical article reports "substantial agreement" of 50,000-100,000 since the beginning of this century, others up to 100,000-200,000. Proposed reasons for inflation include the king's interest in a large army and the ease of overestimating a marching column (Xenophon, Thucydides and Diodorus on counting). These come from forum and magazine pages and a short search summary, not scholarly editions. The Iranica page returned 403. Treat as leads.

## What this means for the scale

My reading, not a sourced finding.

- **Starting level for observational evidence.** A natural experiment or database comparison should not start at "strong" just because it is quasi-experimental. The sources show the usual failure is a hidden assumption (as-if random; "absent" meaning "unknown"), so a rate-down reason like "key assumption untested or contested" fits better than the study's label.
- **Rate-down reasons to name explicitly:** assignment not shown as-if random; comparison group not the one the question needs (Sekhon and Titiunik); missing data re-coded as a real value (moralising gods); non-independent samples with no relatedness control (Galton); focal year or coder mismatch between datasets; dating ambiguity at a curve plateau; single-observer ethnography with no restudy; numbers in old sources with no independent check.
- **Rate-up reasons that appear in the sources:** an experimental benchmark that the design matches (Finnish lottery); pre-treatment balance checks passed (Snow's cholera case); several independent datasets agreeing; archival material that lets a dispute be re-examined (Mead's notes).
- **Contested flag:** the moralising-gods case and the Seshat/DRH disagreements fit a "contested, with named camps and a named test" mark. A "retracted then republished by the same authors" status is a fact a program should record beside the claim.
- **Engine use.** Database-derived claims about causal order (religion before or after complexity) look unsafe for "must respect". Broad descriptive patterns from databases are probably fine for "tunable default". This is a judgment, not tested.
- **Not covered:** how often natural experiments fail overall, ethnographic restudy disagreement rate, Pulotu/DRH/eHRAF coding detail, inference of social structure from remains, and any quantitative reliability of ancient numbers. Another pass would be needed.
