Method: R3 rules. Facts below came from fetched pages unless marked [S] (search snippet only) or "(unverified)". Several publisher pages (ipcc.ch, Springer HTML, PeerJ, PubMed, Nature) returned 403 or login redirects; where that blocked a primary source I say so. No IPCC primary text was read (see Gaps).

## Headline findings

1. Ecology has the same questionable-practice rates as psychology. In a survey of 807 ecologists and evolutionary biologists (15% response rate), 64% admitted not reporting non-significant results, 42% admitted collecting more data after checking significance, and 51% admitted presenting an unexpected finding as if hypothesised ([Fraser et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0200303)). Self-report, low response rate.
2. Direct replication is almost absent. Only 9 of 38,730 open-access ecology/evolution papers (0.023%) call themselves replications. Of 11 claimed pairs, only 2 gave enough statistics for a formal check (3 effects), and 2 of those 3 supported the original ([Kelly 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6743472/)). Ecologists guess the rate is about 10% (median); 97% call replication important ([Fraser et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7319129)). So "replicated" mostly cannot be the top rung for ecology; conceptual repeats in other systems are the normal substitute.
3. Publication bias is large in ecology meta-analyses. Across 87 meta-analyses (4,250 studies, 17,638 effects), correcting for publication bias lowered means by at least 0.12 SD, made 66% (33 of 50) of significant means non-significant, and put average power at 15% with about 4x effect exaggeration (Type M 4.4) ([Yang et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10071700)). A meta-analysis in ecology therefore should not start high without a bias check.
4. Same data, different analysts, different answers. 174 teams (246 analysts) analysed two datasets. Blue tit: average clearly negative, but estimates ran from large negative to near zero, some significant in the opposite direction. Eucalyptus: average near zero, about a third of estimates significant in one direction or the other ([Gould et al., summary page](https://www.conservationgateway.org/collections/people/analytical-variability-ecology-many-analyst-effect-size-study/)).
5. A model can be confirmed only partially. Oreskes et al. 1994: "Verification and validation of numerical models of natural systems is impossible"; models can be confirmed by agreement with observation, but "confirmation is inherently partial," and "the primary value of models is heuristic" ([Oreskes, Shrader-Frechette, Belitz 1994](https://science.whoi.edu/users/mcgillic/12_823/2021/Model_evaluation_May_10/oreskes_etal_94.pdf)).
6. An environmental-evidence grader already exists and is usable. Mupepele et al. 2016 give four levels (LoE1 systematic review to LoE4 no data), with a 43-question checklist that downgrades the level. A model's level depends on testing: untested theory model LoE4, data-parameterised LoE3b, validated LoE3a, confirmed by several unrelated data sets LoE2b. Two assessors on 13 studies agreed only moderately (Cohen's kappa 0.49) ([Mupepele et al. 2016](https://www.biom.uni-freiburg.de/mitarbeiter/dormann/publications/mupepele2016ecolappl.pdf)).
7. Climate ensembles are not equal votes. Roughly 10 of 55 CMIP6 models have climate sensitivity above 5 C and run too hot. AR6 rated models on how well they matched past temperatures instead of averaging all equally ([Climate Foresight summary](https://www.climateforesight.eu/articles/the-hot-model-problem)). Secondary source; the Nature comment itself was not readable.
8. IPCC experts use the two-axis scale loosely. In a survey of IPCC authors (N = 346 clicked the link), confidence rose more with perceived evidence than with agreement, and some authors treat confidence and likelihood as interchangeable ([Kause et al. 2022](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/190055/1/s10584-022-03382-3.pdf)).

## Notes

### Field ecology: how it misleads (Q2 row "Field ecology")

- QRPs: figures in headline 1. Ecologists were less likely than psychologists to report collecting more data after checking significance, and more likely to present unexpected findings as expected. The authors recommend checklists, preregistration/registered reports and open code and data ([Fraser 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0200303)). The 15% response rate and self-reporting limit how far to trust the percentages.
- Replication: figures in headline 2. Kelly counts only studies the authors label as replications. Quasireplication (repeating in other species or systems) is common but is not counted ([search snippet of Kelly, citing Palmer 2000] [S]). In Kelly's data the replicating authors claimed success for 36% of 11 effects, yet the formal check found one claimed failure was not supported ([Kelly 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6743472/)).
- Publication bias and low power: headline 3. The same paper recommends high-power collaborative designs, replication, bias tests and open practice ([Yang 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10071700)).
- Analyst variability: headline 4. Variable selection, random-effects structure and peer-review ratings did not explain how far results strayed from the mean. The page does not give the counts of positive/negative/zero results or exact ranges ([Gould summary](https://www.conservationgateway.org/collections/people/analytical-variability-ecology-many-analyst-effect-size-study/)). Final publication is reported as BMC Biology 2025 [S].
- Short vs long-term studies: Lindenmayer, Likens and colleagues (2012) say long-term datasets are exceptions because studies outlast funding cycles and government terms [S]. I found no verified share of studies under 3 years (unverified). A Krebs blog post claims most journal ecology is short because of degree timelines; it is an opinion piece [S]. LTER-specific material was not found or fetched.
- Citizen science: iNaturalist and eBird are the usual sources. A fetched analysis of 1,107,224 iNaturalist bird records and 255,727,592 eBird records (contiguous US, 2010 to May 2019) found large-bodied birds (beta 0.049, 95% CI 0.023 to 0.073) and birds in large flocks (0.051, 0.034 to 0.069) over-represented in iNaturalist; colour showed no effect. The authors say photo-based records favour detectable, photographable species, and body size can be a covariate ([Callaghan et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8463711)). Weekend, night and winter effort patterns and road/trail clustering come from other papers in a search [S].
- eDNA: simulations show even low false-positive rates bias occupancy and detectability estimates, and manually discarding single PCR detections also biases them. The fix uses prior information or a subset of sites surveyed by a method with no false positives ([Lahoz-Monfort et al. 2016](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/statistical-approaches-to-account-for-false-positive-errors-in-en/), abstract paraphrase). A 2021 review saying eDNA is error-prone and best combined with classical surveys is [S] only.
- Worked contested case, Yellowstone wolves: Ripple et al. (2025) claimed a 1,500% rise in willow crown volume computed from plant height. MacNulty and Cooper's comment (Global Ecology and Conservation, 13 Oct 2025) calls this circular because height was both input and predictor, notes unmatched plots between 2001 and 2020, and argues the response is "more modest and spatially variable," shaped by hydrology and browsing. Hobbs et al. (2024), 20 years of field data, found only weak cascade effects ([EurekAlert release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1102790); a press release, so partisan on one side). Coverage says Hobbs et al. used 8 fenced plots, and that cougars killed elk at about twice the wolf rate [S, news summaries]. Both sides say predator effects are real but context-dependent. Good candidate for a "contested" test claim.

### Grading frameworks used in environmental science (Q1, Q2)

- Mupepele et al. 2016: LoE1 (a systematic review, b ordinary review), LoE2 (controlled: a before-after-control-impact or case-control; b multiple lines of moderate evidence, needing at least two unrelated, consistent arguments), LoE3 (a inferential observational, b descriptive, c multiple weak lines), LoE4 (no underlying data: expert opinion, mechanism-only reasoning). Checklist score maps to downgrades: 75-87% drops half a level, 62-74% one level, 50-61% one and a half, 37-49% two, 25-36% two and a half, under 24% three. "Method" is distinguished from "design": remote sensing can be LoE3b or LoE2a depending on design. Example: a before-after-control-impact study scoring 17 of 25 (68%) fell from LoE2a to LoE3a. The authors note the IPCC combines evidence and agreement and say their tool is more detailed about how to assess evidence; they also admit hierarchies are criticised as rigid and that observational fields (conservation, global theories with no second Earth) stay lower ([Mupepele 2016](https://www.biom.uni-freiburg.de/mitarbeiter/dormann/publications/mupepele2016ecolappl.pdf)).
- Collaboration for Environmental Evidence: CEESAT rates reviews (not primary studies) per criterion from red (poor) to gold (high); one red does not mean the findings are unsupported unless it is for a crucial element such as eligibility criteria [S, search snippet; the CEE page returned 403].
- IPCC (second-hand via Kause et al. 2022): confidence uses five terms (very low to very high), rising with robust evidence (multiple, consistent, independent lines of high-quality research) and with agreement (well established rather than competing or speculative). Likelihood is a separate scale from "exceptionally unlikely" (0-10%) to "virtually certain" (99-100%). The guidance note is unclear on how to combine evidence and agreement, does not recommend confidence statements for every kind of evidence, and one suggestion is to give confidence only when evidence is robust and agreement high. Use of "high confidence" has grown since 1990, and use is inconsistent across working groups ([Kause 2022](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/190055/1/s10584-022-03382-3.pdf)). Another search says the note pairs likelihood only with high or very high confidence [S, not confirmed from the note].

### Models and simulations (Q2 row "Models")

- Oreskes 1994: see headline 5. Reasons given: natural systems are never closed; model results are non-unique (different models can fit the same data). The text argues agreement with data does not prove a model (affirming the consequent) ([Oreskes 1994](https://science.whoi.edu/users/mcgillic/12_823/2021/Model_evaluation_May_10/oreskes_etal_94.pdf)). One critic's NASA report disputes the paper [S].
- Pattern-oriented modelling: the abstract says no standard framework existed for designing, testing and analysing agent-based models, and offers "pattern-oriented modeling" as a unifying strategy ([Grimm et al. 2005 abstract, USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/pattern-oriented-modeling-agent-based-complex-systems-lessons-ecology)). Details (several observed patterns at different scales constrain structure and parameters; choose among candidate models by how many patterns they reproduce) are from search summaries and a student summary [S]; I did not read the full text.
- ODD protocol: seven elements in the 2020 version: purpose and patterns; entities, state variables and scales; process overview and scheduling; design concepts; initialization; input data; submodels. Element 1 must state the purpose and the patterns used as qualitative-but-testable criteria for fitness for purpose, with the reason each pattern was chosen. The article points to a separate format, TRACE, for deeper evaluation documentation. It is cited 2,258 times (2006 paper) and 1,783 times (2010 update) as of Nov 2019 and use grew mostly in life sciences ([Grimm et al. 2020, JASSS](https://www.jasss.org/23/2/7.html)). This gives a ready-made field for "what patterns was this model checked against."
- Ensembles: see headline 7. AR6 weighted by historical skill and, per that summary, did not exclude hot models outright ([Climate Foresight](https://www.climateforesight.eu/articles/the-hot-model-problem)). Independence: Sanderson, Knutti and Caldwell (2015) and Knutti et al. (2017) argue model count overstates independent estimates because models share components, parameterisations and code, and propose down-weighting by similarity [S; search abstracts, the ETH PDF returned no text]. RealClimate is reported to say ensemble-mean skill stops improving after about 10-15 models [S].
- Mupepele's model rungs: see headline 6. This is the only fetched scheme that grades a model by how it was tested.

### Paleoclimate proxies

Only search snippets [S]: tree-ring divergence (northern sites whose growth stopped tracking warming from about the mid-20th century, causes uncertain); a PAGES 2k analysis (Klippel et al., Climate of the Past 2020) found tree rings lack the gradual pre-industrial cooling that ice, marine and lake records show, possibly due to site distribution; PAGES2k v2.0.0 holds 692 temperature-sensitive records, 415 from trees. A reviewer comment disputes the detrending. None fetched; treat as leads.

## What this means for the scale

(My reading, not from the sources.)

- Field ecology and meta-analysis in ecology should start low-to-moderate, not high. Findings 1-4 give concrete downgrade reasons: unreported null results, 15% power, about 4x exaggeration, and analyst variability. A "replicated" flag will rarely be available, so the scale should accept "repeated in other systems" as a weaker, separate mark.
- Mupepele's levels plus a quality-based downgrade map neatly onto a GRADE-style rate-down step. Its kappa of 0.49 is a warning that a two-grader test (Q6) may land near "moderate" agreement unless the scale is simpler.
- For models, record what the model was checked against (the ODD "patterns" idea) rather than calling it validated. A model can feed "must respect" only where its patterns are real data; an untested theory model is at most a shortcut or tunable default.
- IPCC's two axes (evidence, agreement) map onto "grade" plus "contested flag"; the Kause survey shows people blend the axes, so a flag is easier to use than a grid.
- Yellowstone wolves is a good contested test claim: both sides cite data, the dispute is about method (circular volume estimate vs field experiment), and both allow a context-dependent effect. Suggests marking it "contested, effect real but size and generality uncertain" instead of choosing a camp.
