# Letter A: grading systems and the replication literature

Gathered 2026-11-08. Tag key: unmarked = from a page I fetched (WebFetch). [S] = from WebSearch result text only (the search tool returned a summary, I did not open the page). "(unverified)" = not confirmed from anything fetched.

## Headline findings

1. GRADE has four certainty levels (high, moderate, low, very low). Randomized trials start at high, observational studies at low. Five reasons move it down (risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication bias) and three move it up (large effect, dose-response, plausible residual confounding). [GRADE Handbook](https://gdt.gradepro.org/app/handbook/handbook.html)
2. Outside medicine GRADE gets criticised for rating observational evidence too low. Wikipedia reports this critique for nutrition and lifestyle research. [GRADE approach, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GRADE_approach). A 2023 Environmental Health Perspectives paper argues some observational studies can be high-confidence and that GRADE-type frameworks need substantial modification for environmental health [S].
3. Replication rates for published social-science findings sit around 40-60%, with replicated effects well below the originals. OSC 2015: 36% of replications significant vs 97% of originals, effects about half the size. Camerer 2016 (economics): 11 of 18 (61%), effect 66% of original. Camerer 2018 (Nature/Science): 13 of 21 (62%). SCORE 2026: median 49.3% over 13 success criteria, median r 0.25 original vs 0.10 replication. Sources: [Replication crisis, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replication_crisis); [COS SCORE page](https://www.cos.io/score); OSC, Camerer 2016 and 2018 detail and SCORE rate range [S, see Sources].
4. Registered reports report far fewer positive results: 44% vs 96% in standard reports (Scheel et al. 2021). 71 registered-report papers vs 152 standard papers [S].
5. Meta-analyses overstate effects relative to big preregistered multi-lab replications: meta-analytic and replication effects differed significantly in 12 of 15 pairs, meta-analytic effects almost three times as large on average, and three bias-correction methods did not substantively fix it. [Kvarven et al. abstract (IDEAS/RePEc)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nathum/v4y2020i4d10.1038_s41562-019-0787-z.html)
6. That gap is disputed. Lewis et al. 2022 say the gap is smaller once effect heterogeneity is considered, and the two estimates correlate r = 0.72 across the pairs (the methods agree on rank order, not size). [Lewis et al. 2022 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8864345)
7. Publication-bias correction methods disagree and none is safe. In a simulation by Carter et al. cited in a write-up by one of its authors, naive random-effects and trim-and-fill had false-positive rates near 100% under bias, PET/PEESE errors could not be predicted in direction or size, p-curve/p-uniform failed under heterogeneity. [Meta-showdown Part 1, r-bloggers](http://www.r-bloggers.com/?p=151082)
8. IPCC runs two separate scales: a five-level qualitative confidence scale (very low to very high) that combines evidence and agreement, and a quantified likelihood scale (virtually certain 99-100%, very likely 90-100%, likely 66-100%, about as likely as not 33-66%, unlikely 0-33%, very unlikely 0-10%, exceptionally unlikely 0-1%). [Carbon Brief explainer](https://www.carbonbrief.org/explainer-what-the-new-ipcc-report-says-about-extreme-weather-and-climate-change)

## Notes

### Q1: how many levels, one axis or two

- GRADE (one axis, four levels). Definitions: high = very confident the true effect is close to the estimate; moderate = likely close but could be substantially different; low = confidence limited, true effect may differ substantially; very low = very little confidence. [GRADE Handbook](https://gdt.gradepro.org/app/handbook/handbook.html)
- GRADE start rule: "randomized trials without important limitations provide high quality evidence"; "observational studies without special strengths or important limitations provide low quality evidence." Same source.
- GRADE rate-down: risk of bias; inconsistency (unexplained heterogeneity); indirectness (evidence does not match the population, intervention or outcome of the question); imprecision; publication bias. The handbook extract I read did not define the last two. Same source. A trainer deck says publication-bias assessment needs at least 10 studies and downgrades at most one level [S, Cochrane training deck via search]. Usual practice is one or two levels per domain [S].
- GRADE rate-up: large magnitude of effect (one or two levels); dose-response gradient (one level); plausible residual confounding that would shrink a shown effect, or create one where none was seen (one level). Factors are additive. Same handbook page. Whether upgrading is allowed only when nothing was downgraded was not stated in the part I read (unverified). A Cochrane document says upgrading is usually not used for RCT evidence [S].
- GRADE's own critics: Wikipedia says critics hold that for nutrition, diet and environmental exposures, GRADE favours RCTs and rates observational studies low because of confounding, and that this ignores RCT limits for long-term effects. The page says the GRADE Working Group has published guidance on observational studies for long-term effects. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GRADE_approach)
- IPCC (two axes plus a separate probability scale). Confidence has five levels (very low, low, medium, high, very high) and "combines assessments of evidence and agreement"; "established fact" is used when evidence is overwhelming and no qualifier is applied. The Carbon Brief page does not say how evidence and agreement map to each confidence level. [Carbon Brief](https://www.carbonbrief.org/explainer-what-the-new-ipcc-report-says-about-extreme-weather-and-climate-change). Separate search-result summaries say: evidence is graded limited/medium/robust and agreement low/medium/high [not fetched, the IPCC guidance-note PDF returned 403] (unverified as to the exact grid); confidence "should not be interpreted probabilistically"; and a 2022 Climatic Change survey of AR6 authors found the guidance "somewhat unclear" about integrating evidence and agreement, with some authors treating confidence and likelihood as interchangeable [S]. The survey paper itself would not parse (PDF) so I have no numbers from it.
- Maryland Scientific Methods Scale (design-strength ladder, 1 to 5). From a fetched page: level 3 = "Separate comparison group present but non-randomly constituted" with pre-treatment equivalence data; level 4 = separate comparison group present with only minor group differences; level 5 = "Random assignment to comparison and treatment groups." The Evidence-Based Policing Matrix maps its Moderate / Rigorous / Very Rigorous to SMS 3 / 4 / 5 ("similar to", not identical) and excludes levels 1-2. [CEBCP Matrix methods key](https://cebcp.org/evidence-based-policing/the-matrix/inclusion-criteria-methods-key/). Level 1-2 definitions were not on that page. A search summary says level 1 is a cross-sectional correlation, that the 1997 Maryland report judged a program "working" with at least two high-quality studies agreeing, and that Hope (2005) criticises the scale for community-level crime prevention [S, and the level-1 wording is from the search tool's background knowledge, so treat as (unverified)].
- Oxford CEBM levels: not retrieved (cebm.ox.ac.uk returned 403, Wikipedia page 404). (unverified, no content from me)
- Collaboration for Environmental Evidence appraisal tool: not retrieved. The CEE site returned 403. A search found CEESAT (a checklist for appraising evidence reviews/overviews, v2.1 updated August 2025) but nothing on a primary-study tool, so the tool the proposal names may be a different name (unverified). [S]

### Q2 rows: replications and registered reports

- OSC 2015 (100 psychology studies, three journals): 36% of the 97 originally significant effects replicated; replication effect sizes averaged half the originals; of non-replications, 25% directly contradicted and 49% were inconclusive; replication samples about 40% the size of originals; cognitive psychology replicated about 50%, social psychology about 25%. [Wikipedia: Replication crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replication_crisis). Abstract-level cross-check from university repositories: 97% vs 36% significant; 47% of original effect sizes inside the replication's 95% CI; 39% subjectively rated as replicated; success better predicted by strength of original evidence than by team characteristics [S, OSC abstract via repository pages].
- This shows "replicated" is not one thing: 36% (significance), 47% (original inside the replication CI) and 39% (subjective) were all reported for the same project.
- Camerer 2016 economics: 18 lab experiments (AER, QJE 2011-2014), at least 90% power; 11 (61%) significant in the same direction; mean replicated effect 66% of the original; four other indicators gave 67-78%; forecasters expected about 71-75% [S]. Wikipedia figure (61%, 66%) matches. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replication_crisis)
- Camerer 2018: 21 papers in Nature and Science 2010-2015; about 62% reproduced per Wikipedia. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replication_crisis). Search-result detail: 13 of 21; other criteria 57-67%; replication effects on average about 50% of original; prediction markets called 18 of 21 correctly; replication samples about five times larger [S]. A news write-up in the same results gave a different effect-size figure (about 75%), so the effect-size ratio is only abstract-supported (unverified by me).
- Many Labs 2 (Klein et al. 2018): 28 findings, 125 samples, 15,305 participants, 36 countries; 15 (54%) significant in the original direction at p < .05, 14 (50%) at p < .0001; median Cohen's d 0.60 original vs 0.15 replication; 7 larger and 21 smaller than the original; heterogeneity across samples was small and variation was "attributable more to the effect being studied than to the sample or setting." [S]. Wikipedia reports a Many Labs project where 50% of 28 findings failed to replicate (it does not say which Many Labs). [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replication_crisis). Many Labs 1 and 3 not retrieved.
- SCORE (3,900 claims from 2009-2018 social-science papers, 865 collaborators, released April 2026 in Nature): about half precisely reproduced and about three-quarters approximately reproduced; about half of claims replicated with significant same-direction results; median replicated effect size more than 50% below the original; explained variance down more than 80%; about a third of robustness re-analyses reproduced within narrow tolerance. [COS SCORE](https://www.cos.io/score). Search-result detail: 274 claims from 164 papers in the replication paper; median 49.3% across 13 success criteria (range 28.6-74.8%); median r 0.25 original vs 0.10 replication; discipline range about 43-63%; experts about 75% accurate at predicting replication [S]. Reproduction (same data) from a Stirling write-up: 600 quantitative papers, 62 journals; 24% shared data, 20% data and code; 72% reproduced at least approximately and 53% precisely; with data and code 88% approximate and 75% precise; only 11% precise when data had to be reconstructed. [Stirling news](https://www.stir.ac.uk/news/2026/april-2026-news/landmark-study-shows-data-sharing-key-to-reproducibility-across-social-and-behavioural-sciences/). Note: this is a university press item, not the paper.
- Registered reports, Scheel, Schijen and Lakens 2021: first hypothesis in each paper, 96% positive in standard reports vs 44% in registered reports; 50% vs 96% when direct replications are excluded; 71 registered-report papers, 152 standard papers. The authors say the study did not directly test whether registered reports reduce bias, and did not compare effect sizes. [S, several mirror pages including FORRT and TU/e repository; the journal page itself returned 403.]
- Secondary-source point: the meta-showdown authors say naive random-effects is the best choice only when there is no publication bias, "such as in registered reports." [r-bloggers](http://www.r-bloggers.com/?p=151082)

### Q2 rows: meta-analyses

- Kvarven, Strømland and Johannesson 2020: 15 meta-analyses compared with large preregistered multi-lab replications in psychology; 12 of 15 pairs differ significantly; on average meta-analytic effects almost three times as large; three bias-correction methods did not substantively improve things. [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nathum/v4y2020i4d10.1038_s41562-019-0787-z.html). The 2019 preprint cited 17 meta-analyses and said the Andrews-Kasy estimator moderately reduced the inflation [S]; the published version is what the abstract above reports.
- Lewis, Mathur, VanderWeele and Frank 2022 (comment): in a sensitivity analysis of 13 pairs, a worst-case estimate (keep only non-affirmative studies) averaged about 32% of the naive estimates (d 0.17 vs 0.39). Even then 62% of the 13 meta-analyses stayed above the multi-lab estimate, and 92% were not reduced to zero. They conclude publication bias alone is an implausible full explanation and point to effect heterogeneity (small method differences changed one series from d 0.17 to 0.71), standardised vs tailored materials, sample and exclusion choices, and intervention fidelity. They also say a median of about 20% of population effects in each meta-analysis were at least as small as the multi-lab mean. [Lewis et al. (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8864345)
- Reading of the two together: the three-times figure is robust as a gap, but its cause is contested (publication bias vs real heterogeneity vs lab-protocol differences).
- Bias-correction method behaviour (author-run simulation, secondary write-up; crossing true effect 0/0.2/0.5/0.8, heterogeneity, 10-100 studies, 0/60/90% publication bias, questionable research practices): naive RE and trim-and-fill have false positive rates "approaching 100%" under bias; p-curve and p-uniform are fine for homogeneous studies but exceed 50% false positives under heterogeneity; PET/PEESE can over- or under-estimate depending on conditions with near-identical output distributions for different truths; 3PSM suggested as the replacement for effect-size estimation. Their advice: don't trust naive RE or trim-and-fill without registered reports; avoid PET/PEESE; set opposite-sign corrected estimates to zero. Caveat from the author: depends on typical psychology sample sizes and effects. [r-bloggers meta-showdown](http://www.r-bloggers.com/?p=151082)
- Cross-check from search summaries on Carter et al. 2019 (7 estimators, 432 conditions): trim-and-fill and naive RE worst; PET-PEESE and 3PSM better Type I control but lower power; no single method recommended, sensitivity analysis advised; in the ego-depletion data the selection model gave d about .3 and PET-PEESE about -.3 [S]. Same conclusion (methods disagree), different source type.

### Q2 rows: natural experiments / databases

Not in my scope (letter B). Only relevant overlap: the Maryland SMS and GRADE both rank design strength, with randomization at the top and comparison-group quasi-experiments in the middle.

### Use outside medicine

- Environmental health: a Navigation Guide adaptation of GRADE, and the US NTP OHAT approach adopted GRADE in 2013, with case studies on human and animal evidence. Morgan et al. 2016 flag the difficulty of combining human, animal, in vitro and in silico evidence. A 2023 EHP paper says some observational studies can be high-confidence. A 2025 GRADE paper extends the evidence-to-decision framework to environmental and occupational health. No result addressed ecology. [S]
- Criminology: SMS is used in the Evidence-Based Policing Matrix. [CEBCP](https://cebcp.org/evidence-based-policing/the-matrix/inclusion-criteria-methods-key/)

## What this means for the scale

My own reading, not source claims.

- A four-level certainty ladder (high / moderate / low / very low) with the GRADE-style "start by design, then adjust" logic is the proven base. For the game, a short list of down/up reasons will do.
- The replication numbers give usable defaults: for a single published lab or survey result in social science, treat the headline effect as about half the reported size (OSC, SCORE) and the result as roughly a coin flip to reproduce (36-62% across projects). The engine can shrink effect sizes rather than only flipping claims true or false.
- Registered reports (44% positive vs 96%) suggest "preregistered" is a real up-move and "single unregistered positive finding" a down-move. Meta-analysis without registered studies should not automatically start above a big multi-lab replication; Kvarven found the replication was the lower, and arguably less biased, benchmark, but Lewis shows that is contested.
- Two axes seem worth keeping, as IPCC does: how strong the evidence is, and how much the field agrees. The likelihood scale is a separate thing (a probability), probably unnecessary for a game.
- "Replicated" needs a definition in the scale: same direction, significant, and within a stated size range are different tests with different pass rates (36% / 39% / 47% in OSC).
- Gaps I could not close: OCEBM levels, the CEE appraisal tool, the IPCC evidence-by-agreement grid text, Maryland SMS levels 1-2, Many Labs 1 and 3, and anything from the primary Science / Nature / PNAS pages (all 403).
